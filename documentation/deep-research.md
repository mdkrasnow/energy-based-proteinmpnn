# Hybrid ProteinMPNN + IRED Approach: Feasibility and Research Roadmap

Introduction: ProteinMPNN vs. IRED – Current State of the Art

ProteinMPNN (Protein Message Passing Neural Network) is a leading model for structure-conditioned protein sequence design. Given a fixed protein backbone structure, it uses a graph neural network (message passing on the residue contact graph) and an autoregressive decoder to predict a plausible amino acid sequence. Trained on thousands of native protein structures (with some noise-perturbed examples), ProteinMPNN excels at producing sequences that “look like nature” – it achieves ~52% native sequence recovery on test backbones, far surpassing Rosetta’s ~33% recovery
frontlinegenomics.com
. It has shown impressive experimental success in designing soluble, well-folded proteins across various folds, single and multi-chain complexes
frontlinegenomics.com
. However, its training objective is essentially maximizing the likelihood of natural-like sequences given the structure. In other words, ProteinMPNN is biased toward sequences on the natural protein manifold, rather than explicitly optimizing a physics-based stability or energy function. This means MPNN-designed sequences tend to be conservative – excellent at recapitulating known protein features and usually folding correctly, but not necessarily globally optimal in terms of stability or novel functionality outside the evolutionary distribution.

IRED (Iterative Reasoning through Energy Diffusion), by contrast, is a general neural reasoning framework that casts problem-solving as iterative optimization on learned energy landscapes. Rather than one-shot prediction, IRED learns an energy function $E_\theta(x)$ over solution space (here, sequences) and uses gradient-based updates to gradually minimize this energy. Crucially, it adapts the number of optimization steps at inference time based on problem difficulty. This allows IRED to generalize to substantially harder, out-of-distribution (OOD) tasks than those seen during training – for example, solving larger Sudokus or more complex reasoning puzzles by simply running more optimization iterations. Key to IRED’s success are two techniques: (1) learning a sequence of annealed energy landscapes (starting easy, becoming progressively sharper) to guide the optimization, and (2) training with a combination of score matching (gradient) supervision and contrastive energy supervision, which stabilizes training. In essence, IRED can “think longer” on tough problems, whereas a feed-forward model is stuck with whatever it could do in one pass.

Why Combine Them? In protein design, we have a complex, combinatorial optimization goal: find a sequence that minimizes the free energy of folding to a given backbone (and possibly other objectives like binding). ProteinMPNN tackles this indirectly by imitating evolutionary solutions, which yields robust designs but might miss non-natural yet highly stable sequences. An IRED-style energy-based model (EBM) could, in principle, push further: it would learn an energy function that scores sequence candidates by stability, and then iteratively refine sequences to lower that energy. This hybrid would marry ProteinMPNN’s well-honed structural prior (ensuring designs are plausible) with IRED’s iterative optimization (allowing extra computation to explore and escape local optima). The hope is to produce sequences that are more stable and farther from the training distribution than vanilla MPNN designs, and to handle novel design challenges (new folds, multi-objective tasks) with adaptive compute. Below, we explore four key research questions to assess the feasibility of this idea, and we propose a roadmap if it appears promising.

1. Representation & Optimization: Relaxing Discrete Sequences for IRED

Core question: How can we best represent amino acid sequences in a continuous or differentiable way to apply IRED’s energy optimization, and ensure stable, convergent gradient-based updates at inference?

Protein sequences are inherently discrete (20 amino acid categories per position), which poses a challenge for gradient-based optimization. Several approaches could be compared:

Continuous embeddings with straight-through rounding: Represent each position by a continuous embedding or a probability distribution over amino acids. During forward passes, round or sample to a specific amino acid (ensuring a valid sequence), but during backward passes treat the continuous embedding as the variable (the straight-through estimator). This way, gradients propagate in a continuous space, but each iteration still yields a concrete sequence. The ProteinMPNN decoder already produces a logits vector for each position; one idea is to take those logits or their softmax as an initial continuous representation for each residue and then refine them via gradient steps. The straight-through approach was used, for example, in some differentiable protein design pipelines to allow backpropagation through discrete sequence choices.

Gumbel-Softmax relaxation: Use a differentiable approximation of one-hot amino acid vectors. Each position’s amino acid can be encoded as a 20-dimensional softmax that approximates a one-hot; by adding a Gumbel noise and annealing a temperature, one can sample nearly discrete amino acids while still having a differentiable objective (this is effectively a smooth one-hot simplex). Gumbel-Softmax has been successfully applied to sequence generation problems to allow gradient-based optimization and was recently explored for biological sequence design to bridge continuous and discrete domains. The benefit is a principled way to anneal from continuous to discrete; the challenge is that improper temperature scheduling can cause either high-variance gradients (if too discrete too early) or bias (if too soft).

Purely discrete updates with gradient guidance (projected optimization): IRED in discrete tasks (like Sudoku) still leveraged gradient-based updates, effectively by treating the problem in a continuous relaxation and then projecting back to discrete. One could adopt a similar loop for proteins: compute the gradient of the energy wrt a continuous sequence representation, take a step, and then project (e.g. take the argmax amino acid at each position or make a discrete mutation) to get back to a valid sequence before evaluating structural constraints. Alternatively, gradient information could guide which amino acid to mutate at a given position (a form of learned proposal for discrete search). This avoids ever having fractional amino acids, but one must ensure the projection steps don’t lead to oscillation or instability in optimization.

Convergence and quality considerations: The choice of representation will affect optimization dynamics. A continuous relaxation (embeddings or Gumbel-softmax) gives smooth gradients and faster convergence in many cases, as seen in recent work on relaxed sequence optimization where continuous gradient descent drastically accelerated protein design searches. In fact, Frank et al. (2023) note that treating amino acids as differentiable inputs and using backpropagation yields much faster convergence than random mutational walks or MCMC, which struggle with the rugged discrete landscape. However, too loose a relaxation can cause mode collapse to average amino acid frequencies – e.g. the optimizer might settle on fractional mixtures that don’t correspond to any real sequence or yield a bland consensus sequence with low diversity. Straight-through updates or intermittent projection help maintain valid sequences, but they risk non-smooth optimization (sudden jumps when a residue changes identity).

We will need to test sensitivity: Does a Gumbel-softmax with annealing produce diverse solutions or tend to pick one optimal sequence? Does straight-through with an MPNN-initialized embedding converge faster than one initialized randomly? Using ProteinMPNN’s logits as a starting point could ground the search in a good region of sequence space (improving stability), but it might also bias the solution toward a local optimum (the MPNN prediction) and limit exploring truly novel sequences. Perhaps a hybrid strategy works: start with MPNN logits, then gradually increase an exploration noise or decrease a softmax temperature to allow escaping that initial mode if a better (lower-energy) solution exists.

We should also consider the interaction with IRED’s multi-landscape optimization. IRED trains a sequence of energy functions $E_1, E_2, ..., E_T$ of increasing complexity (or decreasing noise). The sequence representation might evolve along this trajectory. For example, early landscapes might allow more continuous (high-temperature) representations, and later landscapes enforce more discreteness as the solution crystallizes. Achieving stable convergence means by the final landscapes the representation should be near-discrete and not oscillating between amino acids. Techniques like gradually sharpening the Gumbel-softmax (lowering temperature across landscapes) or decreasing learning rates can ensure a stable result. Overall, a careful comparison of these representations (embeddings vs. Gumbel vs. direct discrete updates) is needed to see which yields the best trade-off of optimization stability and solution diversity.

Key things to investigate:

Perform ablation experiments with different sequence representations in the iterative optimization loop. Measure how often the optimization converges to a feasible sequence and the quality of that sequence (energy, and perhaps folding prediction).

Examine if certain relaxations lead to low diversity (e.g. always choosing the same amino acids or converging to a single mode). We want to avoid the model only finding trivial or highly conservative solutions.

Test how using ProteinMPNN’s architecture or outputs influences this. For example, using MPNN’s internal logits as the initial state might speed up convergence (fewer steps to find a good sequence) but could also make it harder to discover sequences very different from MPNN’s native-like suggestions.

Monitor whether some approaches get stuck in bad local minima. If purely discrete updates are too greedy, they might need random restarts or momentum; continuous relaxations might glide past some traps but could also overshoot if not careful. IRED’s methodology of annealed landscapes may mitigate some local minima issues by smoothing the landscape initially, but we need to ensure this holds in the protein sequence context.

2. Energy Shaping: Training an EBM to Incentivize Stability OOD

Core question: How should we train the IRED-style energy function (and with what data) so that it truly captures protein stability and other desired properties, rather than just reproducing the ProteinMPNN/native sequence distribution? What “positives” and “negatives” do we present during training to shape the energy landscape?

A standard conditional EBM approach would be to train an energy function $E_\theta(\text{sequence} \mid \text{backbone})$ that assigns low energy to good (stable) sequences and high energy to bad (unstable or misfolding) sequences for each backbone. The challenge is defining these sets in a way that generalizes beyond the training distribution. Key considerations include:

Positive examples (low energy): The most obvious positives are native sequences from the PDB for their corresponding native structures – this was essentially the training data for ProteinMPNN, encouraging the model to favor natural-like sequences. We can certainly use those as baseline positives. However, if we only train on native sequences, the EBM will essentially learn a similar conditional likelihood as MPNN (making the native sequence an energy minimum). To push beyond, we should include other sources of positives that reflect stability even outside evolutionary data. For example:

Designed sequences that are experimentally validated: If available, sequences designed by algorithms (ProteinMPNN, Rosetta, or diffusion models) that were confirmed to fold correctly and are extremely stable could serve as positives. These might include, e.g., sequences from the literature that had high thermal stability or novel folds that were successful. Such data is scarcer but valuable to show the model what “successful out-of-distribution” designs look like.

High-confidence in silico designs: We could take ProteinMPNN-designed sequences and filter them by a stringent criterion such as AlphaFold2 prediction confidence or Rosetta energy. Sequences that pass these tests (e.g., high pLDDT from AF2 indicating the sequence is likely to fold into the given backbone) can be treated as pseudo-positives. Many current pipelines already do this – design a sequence, then verify with AlphaFold or Rosetta and only keep those that look good. Those that pass are presumably more stable (or at least foldable) than the average random sequence.

Stability-enhanced mutants: If there is data on specific mutations that greatly stabilize a protein (from directed evolution or deep mutational scans), those mutant sequences (especially ones that are quite different from natural but more stable) could be included. For example, a mutant of a protein that has much higher melting temperature than wild-type, or consensus sequences of protein families (which are often more stable than any individual natural sequence). This kind of data is limited but could be drawn from protein stability databases or recent papers.

Negative examples (high energy): We want the energy model to assign high energy (i.e., recognize as unfavorable) to sequences that do not fold or are less stable on a given structure. Several strategies to generate negatives:

Random sequences for the same backbone: If you take the target backbone and fill it with a random or arbitrary sequence, almost certainly it will not fold into that structure (or into any stable structure). The energy model should give such sequences a high energy. We can generate many such random sequences per backbone (perhaps with slight bias to maintain realistic amino acid composition). This provides a broad contrast: “native or designed = good, random = bad.” However, random sequences are often trivially bad – they may be too easy for the model to identify, and the model might simply learn to distinguish gross outliers (random sequences tend to have mismatched hydrophobic patterns, charge, etc., which an algorithm can pick up).

Adversarial or near-negative sequences: A more informative negative set might be mutational variants of a good sequence that are predicted to disrupt stability. For example, take a known stable sequence and introduce mutations (single or multiple) that in silico predictors flag as destabilizing (high Rosetta energy or low AF2 confidence). These are “hard negatives” – they are close to a positive sequence in sequence space (so they could look plausible), but they break the protein. Training the energy model to assign higher energy to these will teach it subtler signals of instability (like a buried polar residue, or a broken salt bridge).

Failed designs and misfolds: We can also use sequences that were produced by design methods but turned out to be failures. For instance, sequences that ProteinMPNN or Rosetta generated which did not fold as intended (perhaps experimental attempts that were insoluble or had low expression, or cases where AlphaFold predicts an entirely different structure than the target backbone). In the literature, one often sees many designs tried and only a fraction succeed; the failures (if we know them) are valuable negatives: they look somewhat like a plausible sequence, yet they fail the stability test. We might obtain some from supplemental data of design papers or internal records.

Diverse backbone-unrelated sequences: Another negative could be sequences that fold into some structure, but definitely not the given backbone (for example, a sequence known to fold into a completely different fold). However, this might be overly harsh since the backbone is mismatched entirely. It may be more useful to focus on sequences that attempt to fold into the given backbone but fail.

During training, we could use a contrastive loss: for a given backbone, push the energy $E$ down on one or more positives and up on one or more negatives. This could be done with a margin-based ranking loss (energy_positive + margin < energy_negative) or a logistic loss that classifies sequences as good vs bad. The number and difficulty of negatives is important: using just one random negative might not be enough to shape a rich energy landscape, whereas using a handful including some hard negatives might teach the model more. IRED’s training regime in other domains used a combination of denoising (predicting how to correct a corrupted solution) and contrastive losses, which suggests we might simulate “corruptions” of a good sequence and train the energy to recognize them as higher energy. For example, take a known-good sequence, randomly mutate some residues (corrupt it), and train the model that the original is lower energy than the mutated one.

In-distribution vs OOD performance: We need to monitor that our EBM doesn’t overfit to just labeling natural-like sequences as low energy. If we do this wrong, we could end up with an energy function that is basically a regurgitation of MPNN (favoring sequences close to training data). The inclusion of non-natural positives (designed or mutant) and hard negatives is meant to force the model to learn more functional signals of stability (like proper core packing, satisfying hydrogen bonds, etc.) rather than superficial “nativeness.” Ideally, the energy should correlate with actual folding stability. We can test in-distribution performance by checking if the model still assigns low energy to native sequences (it should) and if an argmin energy sequence for a known backbone still looks reasonable (not gibberish). For OOD generalization, we’d test on cases like a completely novel backbone or a very long protein: does the model still identify stable sequences?

Different contrastive setups will likely affect this balance:

A large margin and aggressive negative mining might improve the gap between stable and unstable energies, potentially improving how well the model spots bad sequences. But if overdone, it might penalize sequences that are merely different (reducing diversity). We might see the model assigning high energy to any sequence that doesn’t resemble training positives, which is not what we want.

More negatives per backbone can help the model learn a broader notion of instability. For example, including both random and subtly mutated negatives can cover both “obviously wrong” and “almost right but wrong” cases.

It may be useful to periodically generate new negatives on the fly (hard-negative mining). For instance, as the model improves, we could attempt to find a sequence that the current model erroneously thinks is low-energy but is predicted by external tools to be unstable, and add it to the training set as a negative. This can continually challenge the EBM to improve.

One must also consider that the energy function could be multi-component. Stability is one objective, but perhaps we also care about other properties (solubility, lack of aggregation-prone motifs, etc.). These could be incorporated either as additional terms in the energy (if we know how to compute a score for them) or implicitly through choosing positives that meet those criteria. For example, if we include only experimentally successful designs as positives, those inherently met certain solubility and expression criteria.

In summary, to train the hybrid model, we will likely:

Use native PDB sequences plus a curated set of validated stable designs as positives (to encourage generalizing beyond wild-type sequences).

Generate a variety of negatives (random sequences, in silico “bad” mutants, known failures) to contrast against each positive.

Use a contrastive loss (like maximizing the energy margin) or the diffusion-style denoising loss (predicting how to correct a corrupted sequence) or a combination of both so that the model learns both to score sequences and to suggest improvements.

Evaluate how this training impacts classic metrics: does the model still recover native sequences reasonably (so we haven’t thrown away the evolutionary signal), and does it rank truly stable sequences higher than unstable ones, even for novel structures? This will tell us if we are incentivizing the right generalization (stability energy minimization) rather than just “likelihood of looking natural.”

3. Backbone Prior: Value of ProteinMPNN’s Encoder for Generalization

Core question: Does incorporating ProteinMPNN’s backbone encoding network (the structure-conditioned message passing module) into the EBM improve generalization to novel backbone structures and design challenges? Or would a simpler or differently-trained structure encoder work just as well or better for the EBM’s purposes?

ProteinMPNN’s encoder is a graph neural network that processes the 3D backbone (typically using distances and orientations between residues) and produces contextual embeddings that inform sequence predictions. This encoder was trained on PDB data to capture structural signals relevant to amino acid preferences – for example, it likely learned that a buried, hydrophobic pocket should contain non-polar residues, or that a glycine is often needed at tight turns, etc. Reusing this encoder in an EBM means we start with a rich structural representation out-of-the-box, rather than training a new one from scratch. There are a few angles to explore:

IRED+EBM with ProteinMPNN encoder (frozen vs. fine-tuned): In this scenario, we feed the backbone into the pre-trained MPNN encoder to get per-residue features, and then have the EBM head (which could be an additional network) produce an energy. We can either keep the encoder’s weights fixed (leveraging the exact representations MPNN learned), or allow them to update slightly during EBM training. Freezing has the advantage of not requiring a lot of structural data to retrain and not risking degradation of known good features. Fine-tuning might adapt the encoder to focus more on stability-related features rather than purely mimicking evolution. An interesting hybrid is possible: freeze the lower layers that capture basic geometry, fine-tune higher layers to adapt from “likelihood” features to “stability” features.

IRED+EBM with an alternative encoder: Perhaps a SE(3)-equivariant transformer or Geometric Vector Perceptron (GVP) network could serve as the backbone feature extractor. The advantage of a fresh encoder is it might learn new representations not biased by ProteinMPNN’s training. For example, a transformer could attend globally and potentially pick up on long-range interactions or symmetry in ways a local message pass might not. However, training a new encoder to the level of accuracy of MPNN’s on limited data could be difficult. ProteinMPNN’s success owes partly to the huge PDB training set and careful training; duplicating that effort for a custom EBM might be inefficient. Another alternative is using a pre-trained structure predictor’s embeddings (like using embeddings from AlphaFold’s trunk or ESMFold’s transformer) as input features. Those models are “structure-to-sequence” predictors in a way (they evaluate how well a sequence fits a structure via their internal scoring). In fact, recent design methods like RFdiffusion fine-tuned RosettaFold’s internal representations to guide design
researchgate.net
. Using such pre-trained structural representations could similarly give a head-start.

The key question is: does using MPNN’s encoder bias the model in a helpful way or constrain it undesirably? On one hand, MPNN’s encoder as a prior likely encodes general principles of protein geometry (packing, secondary structure contexts, etc.) that are applicable even to novel folds. This could help the EBM not only excel on familiar types of backbones but also not be completely lost on novel ones – because basic physics don’t change. If we present a backbone that’s unlike anything in PDB, MPNN’s encoder will still produce some sensible features (it will still identify secondary structures, spatial neighborhoods, etc., even if the global topology is new). Those features might allow the energy model to make reasonable predictions (like “this helix surface is exposed, prefer polar residues there”) rather than having to learn from scratch.

On the other hand, MPNN was trained on natural proteins, which means its encoder might implicitly encode evolutionary biases. For example, it might strongly favor sequences that have natural amino acid distributions or motifs. In OOD scenarios, the truly optimal sequence might violate those conventions (e.g., perhaps a highly stable de novo protein uses an atypical pattern of amino acids not commonly seen in nature). If the MPNN encoder is frozen, the EBM might inherit a bias that could hurt finding those exotic solutions – it might assign higher energy to sequences that are simply unconventional. Fine-tuning could alleviate this if the training data includes examples of such solutions (the encoder could adjust to new patterns), but there is a risk of catastrophic forgetting, where fine-tuning on limited data could degrade the encoder’s broader knowledge.

Evaluation on challenging cases:
We should explicitly test different encoder setups on backbones that are far from the training distribution:

Hallucinated scaffolds: Imagine completely de novo structures generated by hallucination methods (with no close analogs in PDB). Will the EBM with MPNN encoder successfully find sequences for them? (This is somewhat analogous to what MPNN+AF2 pipelines already do, but we might push further).

Unusual symmetries or topologies: e.g., a backbone that is perfectly symmetric or a large repetitive fiber – these might not appear in natural data often. Does the encoder handle them? A fresh SE3 Transformer might, in theory, detect the symmetry explicitly, whereas MPNN might just treat it as any other contacts. However, MPNN’s simplicity might still capture enough.

Length extrapolation: ProteinMPNN was trained on typical protein lengths (most under, say, 800 residues). If we ask for a 1500-residue single-chain protein design, does the encoder scale up? The Science 2024 “RSO” work designed proteins up to 1000 amino acids and still used MPNN for sequence design, indicating MPNN’s encoder can be applied to that length (with some success). But extremely long or complex architectures might stress any model. A transformer might degrade or require chunking the structure.

We can do an ablation study:

Train the EBM with the ProteinMPNN encoder (frozen).

Train another EBM from scratch with a simpler encoder (e.g., just a few GNN layers initialized randomly or a small transformer).

Possibly train one that uses the MPNN encoder but randomly shuffles its weights as a control (to see how much pre-training helps).

Compare their performance on both known-backbone tasks (does it at least match MPNN’s accuracy on those?) and on OOD tasks (does it find low-energy sequences or does it fail?).

If the MPNN encoder consistently improves performance on OOD cases (e.g., higher success rate of designs folding, better energy scores), that suggests it provides a beneficial inductive bias – essentially acting like a structural prior learned from evolution. There is precedent for this: diffusion models like RFdiffusion fine-tuned a pre-trained structure model and got much better results than training from scratch, precisely because they leveraged prior knowledge
researchgate.net
. We might see the same with EBM training.

Conversely, if the scratch model does just as well on novel structures, it might imply that the EBM can learn the necessary structural features from the data we give it (especially if we train on a wide variety of backbones, including some synthetic ones). That would be surprising but would suggest the approach is robust even without fancy priors.

One more nuance: Using MPNN’s encoder could implicitly couple the EBM’s behavior to evolutionary “style.” This might help in some multi-objective scenarios – for example, if we actually care about immunogenicity or ease of expression, sequences too far from natural might perform worse, so the bias could be indirectly beneficial. But if our goal is purely stability, we might need to intentionally allow (or even encourage) the model to break some of those rules. Fine-tuning the encoder on stability data could reweight its features (for instance, maybe MPNN encoder strongly favors certain residue patterns in loops because that’s common in nature, but fine-tuning might realize that other patterns can also work if they satisfy hydrogen bonding).

In conclusion, leveraging the ProteinMPNN encoder is likely a good starting point for this hybrid model, offering a strong baseline of structural understanding. The research should verify:

Does freezing vs fine-tuning change the outcomes significantly on test cases?

Are there specific failure modes on OOD backbones when using the MPNN encoder (e.g., does it systematically avoid certain amino acids or sequence motifs that might actually be viable)?

How an alternative encoder (or none at all) compares, to quantify the value of this prior. Given the success of approaches that reuse structure predictors for design
researchgate.net
, we expect the MPNN encoder to be quite helpful in generalizing to new tasks, but it’s important to identify any blind spots it introduces.

4. Empirical Benefit: Iterative Optimization vs. Feed-forward Design

Core question: Given a fixed computational budget, can an IRED-style iterative optimization approach produce protein sequences that are more stable and more robust to distribution shifts than those produced by one-shot models like ProteinMPNN or other generative models? In practice, do additional optimization iterations (especially adaptively applied to hard cases) yield clear gains in stability metrics, or do we hit diminishing returns?

To answer this, we need to define how to measure "more stable" and "more OOD" in a rigorous way. Proposed evaluation scenarios:

Evaluation scenarios (tasks):

De novo backbone designs: Test on protein backbones that were never seen during training – for instance, structures generated by hallucination or new algorithmic design, or even random math-generated shapes. These are true extrapolations. Compare how well different methods can find a sequence that folds into these backbones.

Multi-constraint design problems: For example, design a protein that not only folds into a given structure but also binds to a target or avoids certain motifs. A concrete case: a backbone is given for an enzyme or binder; the sequence must both stabilize that fold and achieve a binding interface or an epitope masking. Another case is designing a protein with two objectives like fold + specific catalytic site geometry. These are harder because they add additional energy terms or constraints.

Extrapolation in size/complexity: e.g., designing an oligomeric assembly of a size far larger than anything in the training set, or a novel topology with many secondary structure elements. For instance, if training saw mostly single domains, test on a multi-domain protein or a huge symmetrical cage. The iterative method can allocate more steps here, whereas a feed-forward might confidently output something that actually fails.

Stability and success metrics:
In silico metrics will be the primary filter before any lab testing:

Folding confidence: Run structure prediction (AlphaFold2 or ESM-Fold) on the designed sequence and see if it confidently folds into the given backbone shape. Metrics like pLDDT (predicted Local Distance Difference Test) or TM-score to the target backbone can quantify success. A higher pLDDT or lower RMSD to the target indicates the sequence is likely folding correctly. Also, for binding tasks, interface metrics like AlphaFold’s predicted aligned error (PAE) at the interface can tell if the designed binder is likely to stick as intended.

Energy calculations: Use Rosetta or similar physics-based tools to compute an approximate folding energy or ΔΔG for folding. A lower (more favorable) energy indicates a more stable protein. Also, Rosetta can estimate solvation energy and other terms to catch sequences that might aggregate.

Secondary structure/solvent exposure agreement: Check if the sequence’s properties match the backbone’s needs (e.g., proportion of hydrophobic residues buried vs exposed). There are simple metrics like how well the designed sequence’s hydropathy pattern aligns with the backbone’s solvent exposure pattern.

Aggregation propensity: Tools like TANGO or Aggrescan can predict if a sequence has regions likely to aggregate. A good design should minimize such regions.

Novelty metrics: To quantify “out-of-distribution,” we can measure sequence identity to the nearest natural sequence or use something like Foldseek to see if the designed structure is similar to any known fold. Also, a language model perplexity on the sequence could tell if it looks like a natural sequence or something quite novel. Truly OOD sequences might look like low-probability outliers to an LLM but still fold well.

Experimental metrics (long-term): If possible, eventually test a few designs in the lab for expression yield, solubility, thermal stability (e.g., melting temperature), and function/binding if relevant. But for a feasibility study, the above computational metrics are a good start.

Methods to compare: We should pit the hybrid approach against several baselines:

ProteinMPNN (standard): For each test backbone, let MPNN generate sequences (it can even generate multiple samples stochastically if we want diversity). This represents the strong feed-forward baseline. We expect it to do well on backbones similar to training ones, but possibly less so on weird ones.

ProteinMPNN + simple gradient fine-tuning: This means after MPNN proposes a sequence, we do a small number of post-hoc optimization steps – for example, run a quick gradient-based refinement using an external score (like AlphaFold’s confidence or a Rosetta energy gradient if one can be obtained). This is not the full IRED approach, just a one-time refinement. It’s similar in spirit to how some protocols use local sequence optimization on top of a generative model output. If this yields improvement, it shows that even a little bit of iterative optimization helps.

Diffusion model baseline: Since diffusion models (like RFdiffusion) are now popular for protein design, we should compare to them on these tasks. Diffusion models perform iterative refinement too, but with a fixed schedule learned during training. They have shown excellent performance on many design problems, sometimes outperforming MPNN on more complex design objectives. However, diffusion typically runs a fixed number of steps (e.g., 50 or 100 denoise steps). We might treat RFdiffusion as a proxy for a non-adaptive iterative method. If our IRED-style method (with adaptive steps) can outperform RFdiffusion on some hard tasks, that’s a strong sign of the benefit of adaptive reasoning.

Non-iterative EBM baseline: If possible, one could train a feed-forward model to predict the argmax sequence given a backbone under a learned energy function (sort of a one-shot prediction of the minimum energy sequence). This is tricky because EBMs usually require solving an optimization at test time. But perhaps a model like a single-step predictor trained to output low-energy sequences (maybe analogous to how one could train a model to directly output the solution of an optimization problem). This baseline might not be easy to construct; alternatively, we could compare to a pure supervised model trained on the same data of “stability” (like a classifier that was given backbone+sequence and predicts “will it fold or not”). However, that wouldn’t produce sequences, just evaluate them. So likely the meaningful comparison is feed-forward generative (MPNN or FrameDiffusion etc.) vs our iterative approach.

Expectations and what to look for:

If the IRED hybrid is working as intended, we anticipate seeing:

On novel backbones, the IRED-based method should produce sequences with higher predicted folding confidence (e.g., better AlphaFold metrics) than ProteinMPNN’s sequences. Perhaps MPNN sometimes fails if the backbone has features it hasn’t seen (leading to design that doesn’t quite pack well), whereas the iterative method can adjust residues to satisfy constraints even if the initial guess was off. An adaptive method can also try more steps or re-initialize if the first attempt doesn’t look good, effectively using more compute to search the sequence space. We might see, for example, the IRED method achieving good folding predictions for, say, 90% of novel backbones, whereas MPNN might only get 70% to fold (numbers hypothetical).

On multi-constraint problems, a feed-forward model might struggle because it wasn’t explicitly trained for that combined objective. For instance, designing a binder: MPNN could design each part separately (target and binder) but doesn’t inherently optimize the interface geometry. Diffusion models have started to address this with techniques like constrained inpainting, but an energy-based approach could naturally include a term for binding energy in the score. We expect IRED to handle these by simply adding e.g. an interface energy term to the total energy and letting the optimizer rip. The result might be sequences that make the intended contacts without falling apart. We’d compare binding metrics (like interface PAE or Rosetta interface score) between methods. Ideally, the hybrid method produces binders with significantly better predicted affinities or success rates. If it can generalize (with more steps) to very challenging targets (maybe ones requiring high shape complementarity), that demonstrates the power of iterative reasoning.

On very large or complex designs, an adaptive iterative method can allocate a larger number of steps. We should see a pattern analogous to IRED’s Sudoku results: for easy cases, maybe both one-shot and iterative succeed similarly; for harder cases, the iterative method’s performance continues to improve as we allow more optimization steps, whereas the one-shot methods hit a ceiling. For example, perhaps designing a 24-mer protein cage: MPNN might output sequences but many might misfold or break symmetry. The EBM, by spending more time, could adjust each subunit’s sequence to ensure the whole assembly is stable, thus yielding a higher proportion of successful designs. If we plot performance vs. computational effort, the feed-forward line is flat (more compute doesn’t help it once it’s done), but the iterative one rises – hopefully showing that with, say, double the iterations, we solve cases that previously failed. This kind of adaptive compute benefit was exactly what IRED demonstrated in puzzles (more steps = solve harder puzzles).

We must also watch for diminishing returns: it could be that after a moderate number of iterations, further improvements are minor. Perhaps the energy landscape for protein sequence is so rough or the model so limited that beyond a point, extra steps don’t help because the model might get stuck in a local minimum (or the energy model might not be perfect and could mislead further search). Part of the IRED training is to learn landscapes that avoid getting the optimizer stuck – effectively, by the time you reach the final landscape, you should already be in the basin of a good solution. We might find that, say, 50 iterations gets you to a good sequence for most tasks, and going to 100 only marginally improves things. If adaptive step count helps, we should see that for particularly troublesome backbones, the model does keep improving with more steps until it finds a solution (or it can report if it cannot find one, which is also useful information – a one-shot model never says “I’m unsure”, it just gives something).

Finally, consider failure modes: If the iterative method ever produces worse sequences than a feed-forward approach for easy cases (in-distribution), that indicates either training issues or that the iterative process over-optimized some proxy at the cost of naturalness. For instance, it might find a sequence that makes AlphaFold happy but is very unnatural and maybe has other issues (like maybe it folds but has a sticky patch that wasn’t penalized). We should evaluate that too – e.g., check if the sequences from the EBM are as or more diverse than MPNN’s (not collapsing to one sequence) and if they avoid new problems (like maybe they fold but are predicted to aggregate due to lots of hydrophobic residues used to maximize stability). The training should include those considerations, but it’s worth keeping an eye on.

In summary, the empirical tests will illuminate whether the hybrid approach truly yields quantitative improvements:

Higher success rates of folding in new scenarios.

More stable energy scores.

Ability to solve design challenges that one-shot models cannot (with the same or slightly more compute).

Graceful scaling to harder problems by expending more iterations (whereas non-adaptive models fail or require retraining).

If these hypotheses hold true, it strongly justifies pursuing the hybrid ProteinMPNN + IRED EBM approach.

Feasibility Analysis & Proposed Research Roadmap

Feasibility: Based on the above considerations, a hybrid approach that combines ProteinMPNN’s structural insights with an IRED-style energy-based optimization appears highly promising. ProteinMPNN has already proven effective for protein design, and IRED has demonstrated the power of iterative, energy-driven reasoning in other domains. The key open questions (representation of sequences, training data for the energy model, use of pre-trained encoders, and empirical advantages) all have plausible solutions or at least clear experimental pathways to resolve. There is no fundamental barrier identified – rather, it is a matter of engineering and research effort to determine the best configurations. The approach aligns with trends in the field: generative diffusion models have shown the benefit of iterative refinement, and recent studies have successfully used gradient-based sequence optimization to push beyond conventional designs. This hybrid would effectively unify these advances. Therefore, it should be pursued as an innovative direction to achieve more robust and general protein design.

Research Roadmap: To systematically develop and evaluate this hybrid model, we propose the following stages:

Representation & Optimization Experiments: Start with a simplified setup to answer Question 1. Implement multiple sequence representation schemes – e.g., (a) continuous softmax with straight-through argmax, (b) Gumbel-softmax with annealing, and (c) discrete mutation steps guided by gradients. Use a small set of test backbones (with a known native sequence for validation) and a simple energy proxy (for initial tests, the negative log-likelihood under ProteinMPNN or a Rosetta score could be used as a stand-in energy). Examine convergence behavior, diversity of solutions, and alignment to known optimum (native sequence or lowest Rosetta energy). From these tests, identify which representation yields stable convergence and good solutions. Milestone: Select a sequence representation/optimization strategy that works well (e.g., Gumbel-softmax annealed across iterations) for further development.

Prototype EBM Training (Energy Shaping): Using the chosen representation, set up a prototype energy model. Begin on a restricted problem for ease: for instance, take a single protein family or a small set of backbones and train the EBM to distinguish the native sequence vs. random and mutated sequences. This will involve implementing contrastive losses. Gradually incorporate more realistic negatives (as in Question 2) once the basics work. Monitor if the energy function can learn to rank sequences correctly by stability. Possibly involve a simple stability predictor (like secondary structure agreement or Rosetta score) to generate negatives. Milestone: EBM can be trained to preferentially score native/designed sequences over obviously unstable ones, indicating it’s capturing meaningful sequence-quality signals beyond just memorizing training sequences.

Integration of ProteinMPNN Encoder (Backbone Prior): Incorporate ProteinMPNN’s structure encoder into the EBM. Initially, use it frozen to generate features for the energy network. Retrain the EBM (as in stage 2) now with these features as input instead of, say, one-hot structural descriptors. Evaluate performance on the same tasks and also on some backbones outside the training set to see if the pre-trained encoder improves generalization. Then try fine-tuning the encoder slightly during training and see if that improves the energy modeling of stability. Possibly compare with a baseline encoder (like a small GNN trained from scratch). Milestone: Demonstrate that using the MPNN encoder yields equal or better performance on in-distribution tests, and noticeably better stability predictions on some OOD backbones, confirming the value of the prior.

Scaling Up Training Data (Positives/Negatives): Expand the training to a diverse set of proteins. Compile a dataset for the EBM: this would include many backbone-sequence pairs labeled as “stable” or “unstable.” Leverage PDB (native pairs as stable), plus create synthetic negatives for each (random sequences, etc.). Also incorporate any experimental design data available (e.g., from recent papers, include some known successful designs as additional positives, and known failures as negatives if available). Implement a strategy for hard negative mining – for instance, periodically run the current EBM (or MPNN) to generate a sequence for a backbone, check it with AlphaFold/Rosetta; if it looks bad (low confidence/high energy), use that as an additional negative example for further training. This stage will likely require substantial computation (many evaluations with folding prediction to label negatives), but it will shape the model’s energy landscape towards what we want. Milestone: Train a robust ProteinMPNN+IRED energy model that can take a backbone and assign low energies to good sequences and high to bad ones, across a wide range of structures. Validate that on a hold-out set of backbones, the native sequence is scored better than random or mutated ones, and that known stable vs unstable mutants are distinguished correctly by the model.

Iterative Inference Testing: With the trained energy model in hand, test the full iterative design loop on various tasks (Question 4 scenarios). Develop a few benchmark sets:

A set of novel single-domain backbones (e.g., hallucinated by RFDiffusion or other methods).

A couple of multi-domain or symmetric assemblies.

Perhaps a binder design scenario (backbone of binder + target structure).
For each, use the hybrid model to generate sequences by iterative optimization (with adaptive step count: allow it to run until either convergence or some max steps, possibly increasing for harder cases). Also generate sequences with baseline methods (MPNN, diffusion). Evaluate all sequences with the in silico metrics discussed (AlphaFold, Rosetta, etc.). Compare outcomes. Milestone: Obtain quantitative results demonstrating how the hybrid model performs relative to baselines. For example, “On 50 novel backbones, hybrid approach achieved high-confidence folds in 40 cases vs 30 for MPNN” or “for a challenging binder, hybrid achieved better interface scores”. These results will highlight strengths and any weaknesses.

Iterative Refinement & Tuning: Based on the benchmark results, iterate on the method. Perhaps the results show that the model struggles with very long proteins – this might mean we need to augment training with more long proteins or tweak the encoder. Or maybe the energy function wasn’t sharp enough to differentiate subtle stability issues – perhaps incorporate an AlphaFold fine-tuned confidence predictor into the training (if not already). This stage is about polishing: adjusting loss weights (for positives vs negatives), adding terms for things like aggregation if needed, tuning the schedule of optimization (number of iterations, annealing scheme) for best results. We would also examine cases where the iterative method didn’t help or made things worse, and troubleshoot why (e.g., did it over-optimize some surrogate metric at the cost of something else?).

Final Evaluation – Feasibility Conclusion: With a refined model, do a larger evaluation, possibly including some laboratory collaboration if feasible. Summarize whether the hybrid approach indeed beats the state-of-the-art on the targeted metrics. For instance, if diffusion or MPNN were baseline, where does our method stand? If the results show clear improvement in stability or success rate on OOD designs, that supports moving forward aggressively. If improvements are marginal, analyze cost vs benefit: it might still be feasible if the hybrid approach offers other advantages (like flexibility to new objectives). Also consider computational cost: iterative methods can be slower – we must ensure that the adaptive approach doesn’t make the design pipeline impractically slow. If it is slower, is the gain in success worth the extra time? Perhaps for difficult tasks it is.

Throughout this roadmap, maintain a feedback loop with experimentalists (if possible): designs coming out of these methods could be tested in small numbers to validate if the predicted “more stable” sequences actually behave better in the real world. Getting even a few data points of experimental confirmation would be a powerful proof of feasibility.

By following this roadmap, we will address each of the four structured questions empirically. At the end, we should have a clear answer on whether a “ProteinMPNN + IRED-style EBM” hybrid can deliver superior results, and a concrete strategy for deploying it. The anticipated outcome is that yes, this hybrid approach is feasible and advantageous, offering improved stability-oriented design especially on novel or complex targets, thereby extending the capabilities of protein design beyond the current state-of-the-art.
